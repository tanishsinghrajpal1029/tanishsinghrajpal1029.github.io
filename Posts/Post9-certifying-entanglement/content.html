Manuscript in preparation (October 2026). This independent study at Carnegie Mellon's Information Networking Institute is with Prof. Michael Mattarock. The code, pre-registrations, results, and evidence ledger are public.

The question

Future quantum networks will deliver entangled pairs for teleportation, cryptography, and distributed computing. A pair has to be measured to inspect its quality, but that measurement consumes it. What can a network honestly promise about the pairs a user receives, and what does each kind of promise cost?

What I found

Per-pair promises need a model; averages do not. A rule that sees only the results of tests cannot guarantee that every pair a user keeps clears a fidelity threshold. The project therefore separates two kinds of evidence:

  • A model-based guard uses calibration counts and anytime-valid confidence sequences to decide which pairs to deliver. Under stated assumptions about the physics and noise, it bounds a user's chance of ever receiving a below-threshold pair, even when routing decisions adapt to earlier observations.
  • A model-free certificate uses a hidden, random fraction of pairs for measurement. Those tests lower-bound the average fidelity of the pairs the user kept without relying on the delivery model. The certificate concerns an average, not every individual pair, and assumes trusted measurements and fresh, hidden test choices.
  • A model check closes the loop. The same tests look for a guard whose predictions are too optimistic. After an alarm, the guard raises its safety margin using an upper bound derived from the users' tests. The post-alarm guarantee holds for the declared class of model errors.

The certificate also has a cost. With 10% of pairs reserved for tests, certifying a 0.9 fidelity target from perfect pairs needs 678 kept pairs in this method, versus a theoretical floor of 330.

How I tested it

Simulated networks. In pre-registered studies on grids up to 30 × 30 nodes (900 nodes and 1,740 links), none of 4.2 million pairs delivered after an alarm fell below the fidelity threshold. With the margin active from the start, none of 7.8 million delivered pairs fell below it, at a cost to throughput. The Python discrete-event simulator's physics was cross-checked against NetSquid, and confirmatory jobs ran on PSC Bridges-2. These are simulation results, not measurements from a distributed quantum network.

Public experimental data. I re-analysed four published experiments: Innsbruck, ICFO, USTC, and Delft. The Innsbruck data yielded a model-free fidelity certificate of at least 0.887 at 95% confidence. The Delft data showed why measurement readout needs to be characterised: the uncorrected counts alone did not certify entanglement, while the authors' readout correction did. The underlying datasets remain with their original publishers.

Quantum hardware. I ran two test-or-keep experiments on one IBM quantum processor. The certificate held in both runs. The first model check missed an injected degradation; a revised check in the second run raised an alarm. These single-device runs test components of the method, not entanglement distribution over distance.

How the project developed

I started with reinforcement-learning policies for routing in noisy quantum grids. Better routing, however, did not tell a user whether the pair they received was good. That led me from throughput to risk control, then to a per-user guard, a model-free average certificate, and a check that feeds test results back into the guard.

The confirmatory studies were pre-registered, with analysis plans and failures kept in the public record. Across the project, 76 of 87 primary hypotheses passed and 11 failed. The repository also records corrections, including a superseded proposition and simulations whose setup inflated detection power. Study 33 is a computation rather than a pre-registered confirmatory study.

Limits and next steps

The model-based guard depends on its declared assumptions; persistent, slowly varying noise is not covered. The model-free certificate assumes trusted measurements and certifies an average rather than each pair. An alarm cannot undo harm before it, and detecting small model errors can be slow. The manuscript is in preparation, and its proofs are undergoing human review. I want to explore faster detection without losing the guarantees and test the method on real network data.
